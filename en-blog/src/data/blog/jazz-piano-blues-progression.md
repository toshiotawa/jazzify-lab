<p>The <strong>jazz piano blues progression</strong> is the shortest path to practicing swing feel, dominant seventh harmony, and improvisation in one form. Jazz musicians use many blues variants, but a basic 12-bar framework in F appears on countless jam sessions. Learn it deeply before chasing rhythm changes.</p>

<p>This article maps the bars, explains chord symbols, shows shell voicing comping, and outlines a beginner soloing approach using chord tones and the F blues scale.</p>

<h2 id="basic-12-bar-form-in-f">Basic 12-Bar Form in F</h2>

<p>A common jazz blues:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Bar 1–4: F7 | Bb7 | F7 | F7</li>
  <li>Bar 5–8: Bb7 | Bb7 | F7 | F7</li>
  <li>Bar 9–12: C7 | Bb7 | F7 | C7</li>
</ul>

<p>Each symbol is a dominant seventh chord built on that root: F7 (F-A-C-Eb), Bb7 (Bb-D-F-Ab), C7 (C-E-G-Bb). Jazz treats every chord as dominant quality even when classical theory might call I a major triad—the b7 is essential to the sound.</p>

<h2 id="why-all-dominants">Why All Dominants?</h2>

<p>Blues harmony predates functional major-key rules. The b7 on F7 (Eb) gives the mixolydian color that defines the genre. When you move to Bb7, the same quality continues the groove rather than asserting a classical IV major.</p>

<p>If you need seventh-chord construction review, start with <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-chords-for-beginners/">jazz piano chords for beginners</a>.</p>

<h2 id="shell-voicing-comping">Shell Voicing Comping</h2>

<p>Left-hand shells on beats 1 and 3:</p>

<ul>
  <li>F7: F-Eb (root-7th);</li>
  <li>Bb7: Bb-Ab;</li>
  <li>C7: C-Bb.</li>
</ul>

<p>Right-hand Charleston: chord on 1, staccato chord on and-of-2. Play four choruses without soloing until the form is automatic. Say roots aloud while playing: “F, Bb, F, F…”</p>

<h2 id="turnaround-variations-in-bars-11-12">Turnaround Variations in Bars 11–12</h2>

<p>Many charts use a ii-V into F in bars 11–12: Gm7-C7 instead of F7-C7, or quick ii-V-I: Gm7 | C7 | F7 | C7. Jam sessions often agree verbally—know both.</p>

<p>The ii-V pair Gm7-C7 is a direct application of the <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-ii-v-i-progression/">ii-V-I progression</a> fragment resolving to F7 (I7 in blues). Voice-lead: Bb and F on Gm7 to B and F on C7.</p>

<h2 id="left-hand-bass-patterns">Left-Hand Bass Patterns</h2>

<p>Roots on beat 1 only for week one. Week two: root-fifth-root-fifth. Week three: walking half notes F-C | Bb-F | F-C | F-A | etc. Our <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-left-hand-bass/">left-hand bass guide</a> expands these patterns.</p>

<h2 id="blues-scale-for-first-solo">Blues Scale for First Solo</h2>

<p>F blues scale: F-Ab-Bb-B-C-Eb-F (1-b3-4-b5-5-b7-8). Use it sparingly—start phrases on F, Ab, or C on downbeats aligned with F7 or Bb7 changes.</p>

<p>Chorus 1 rule: only three notes per bar, blues scale allowed but land on F, A, or C on F7 bars. Chorus 2: add chord tones Eb on F7 (the b7) and Ab on Bb7.</p>

<h2 id="mixolydian-alternative">Mixolydian Alternative</h2>

<p>F mixolydian on F7: F-G-A-Bb-C-D-Eb-F. Sounds less “blues cliche,” more bebop. Try mixolydian on C7 in bar 9 leading back to F7—emphasize Bb and E (4th and 13th) as passing tones.</p>

<h2 id="rhythm-and-session-etiquette">Rhythm and Session Etiquette</h2>

<p>Blues jam etiquette: keep comping steady, do not walk over a soloist’s range, signal endings with a raised foot or nod—outside piano scope but worth knowing. Rhythmically, lock to 2 and 4; our <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-rhythm-practice/">rhythm practice article</a> applies directly.</p>

<h2 id="common-jazz-blues-substitutions-later">Common Jazz Blues Substitutions (Later)</h2>

<p>Advanced charts add #IV diminished (Bdim7) in bar 2, secondary dominants, or I-VI-ii-V turnarounds. Ignore these until the basic form is memorized. Substitutions are covered in <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/secondary-dominant-chords/">secondary dominant chords</a> when you are ready.</p>

<h2 id="transpose-to-other-keys">Transpose to Other Keys</h2>

<p>Gig-friendly keys: F, B-flat, E-flat, C. After F is solid, move to B-flat: Bb7-Eb7-Bb7… Same structure, new fingerings. Transposing prevents “I only know blues in one key” syndrome.</p>

<h2 id="four-week-plan">Four-Week Plan</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Week 1: roots + form memory;</li>
  <li>Week 2: shell comping with Charleston;</li>
  <li>Week 3: blues scale solo, three notes per bar;</li>
  <li>Week 4: add turnaround ii-V and mixolydian on C7.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="bebop-blues-variant">Bebop Blues Variant</h2>

<p>Jazz blues often adds a ii-V in bar 4: F7 | Bb7 | F7 | Gm7 C7 | continuing into bar 5. The Gm7-C7 in bar 4 approaches Bb7 with stronger forward motion. Shell-comp the added ii-V with Gm7 (G-F, Bb-F) into C7 (C-Bb, E-Bb) before landing Bb7.</p>

<h2 id="minor-blues-contrast">Minor Blues Contrast</h2>

<p>Compare F major blues dominants with F minor blues (Fm7-Bb7-Eb7-Ab7 chain in some forms). Minor blues uses m7 and 7 alternation; solo with F minor pentatonic (F-Ab-Bb-C-Eb) instead of major blues scale. Hearing the shift prevents playing major-third lines on minor blues gigs.</p>

<h2 id="trading-fours-on-the-blues">Trading Fours on the Blues</h2>

<p>With a practice partner or app, trade four-bar solos over F blues: four bars comping, four bars soloing chord tones only. Keeps form tight and simulates jam session etiquette where pianists comp until their solo chorus.</p>

<h2 id="chromatic-approach-notes-in-the-bass-line">Chromatic Approach Notes in the Bass Line</h2>

<p>When walking quarter notes on F7 to Bb7, approach Bb from A or Bb from below with Ab on beat 4 of the F7 bar. Chromatic approaches are optional spice—use one per four bars at first so the line stays diatonic enough for beginners to analyze.</p>

<h2 id="counting-form-aloud">Counting Form Aloud</h2>

<p>Say “bar 1” through “bar 12” while playing roots only. Hesitation on bar 9 reveals turnaround confusion before you add voicings. Many beginners know bars 1–8 but stumble when C7 arrives in bar 9—count every chorus until numbers are automatic.</p>

<h2 id="shell-voicings-on-every-dominant">Shell Voicings on Every Dominant</h2>

<p>F7 shell F-Eb, Bb7 Bb-Ab, C7 C-Bb—practice the full twelve bars with left-hand shells and metronome on 2 and 4 before adding right-hand Charleston. Dominant seventh quality is uniform in basic jazz blues; muscle memory transfers to any key once F is clean.</p>

<h2 id="listening-list-for-blues-feel">Listening List for Blues Feel</h2>

<p>Hear Wynton Kelly on “Tenor Madness,” Oscar Peterson trios on blues at medium tempo, and a slow B-flat blues for dynamic contrast. Copy comping density—not notes—from one recording per week. Your voicings stay yours; the rhythm reference keeps time honest.</p>

<h2 id="transpose-to-b-flat-before-the-jam">Transpose to B-Flat Before the Jam</h2>

<p>F blues is pedagogical default; many sessions call B-flat. Bb7-Eb7-Bb7… same dominant shells transposed: Bb-Ab, Eb-Db, F-Eb. Drill B-flat the week before any jam announcement so you are not learning roots mid-performance.</p>

<h2 id="right-hand-riffs-on-dominant-chords">Right-Hand Riffs on Dominant Chords</h2>

<p>After shells lock, add a two-note pickup into bar 1: on F7, play A and C on the and-of-4 of the previous bar leading to F on beat 1. Pickups teach anticipation without leaving beginner chord-tone vocabulary.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>12-bar jazz blues in F: F7-Bb7-F7, Bb7-Bb7-F7-F7, C7-Bb7-F7-C7.</li>
  <li>All chords are dominant sevenths with shell voicings.</li>
  <li>Learn basic form before substitutions.</li>
  <li>Solo with blues scale and chord tones on downbeats.</li>
  <li>Transpose to B-flat and E-flat for session readiness.</li>
</ul>
