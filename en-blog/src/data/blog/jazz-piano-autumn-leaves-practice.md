<p><strong>Autumn Leaves</strong> is a rite of passage for jazz pianists. Its 32-bar form moves between relative major and minor keys with clear ii-V-I cadences—ideal for shells, guide-tone comping, and a first improvised chorus built only on chord tones.</p>

<p>This guide covers the common keys, a bar-by-bar harmonic outline (no copyrighted melody notation), left-hand strategies, comping rhythms, and a stepwise solo plan.</p>

<h2 id="form-overview">Form Overview</h2>

<p>Autumn Leaves uses a 32-bar AABC structure. Each section is eight bars. Harmonic rhythm is mostly one chord per two beats in the A sections, with ii-V-I chains dominating. You will see the progression in G minor or E minor on many gigs; both are worth preparing.</p>

<h2 id="harmony-in-g-minor-concert">Harmony in G Minor (Concert)</h2>

<p>A section (bars 1–8), simplified symbols:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cm7 | F7 | Bbmaj7 | Ebmaj7 |</li>
  <li>Am7b5 | D7 | Gm7 | Gm7 |</li>
</ul>

<p>The first four bars move through ii-V-I toward B-flat major (Cm7-F7-Bbmaj7), then a parallel ii-V-I toward E-flat major (Dm7-G7-Ebmaj7—often written with minor ii and dominant V). Bars 5–8 return toward G minor: Am7b5-D7-Gm7.</p>

<p>B section (bars 9–16) typically repeats the same harmonic story with slight variation in the last two bars leading to the bridge.</p>

<p>C section (bars 17–24) is the bridge—often sequential ii-V-I motion through keys a third apart, frequently including Bbmaj7 and Am7b5-D7 again before returning to G minor.</p>

<p>Final eight bars resolve the form back to G minor with another cycle through the A material.</p>

<p>Consult your lead sheet for exact passing chords; charts vary slightly by edition.</p>

<h2 id="harmony-in-e-minor">Harmony in E Minor</h2>

<p>Many vocal versions use E minor. The relationships are identical a minor third lower:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Am7 | D7 | Gmaj7 | Cmaj7 |</li>
  <li>F#m7b5 | B7 | Em7 | Em7 |</li>
</ul>

<p>Practice both keys once G minor is comfortable. Transposing teaches the <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-ii-v-i-progression/">ii-V-I progression</a> as a movable template, not a single grip in one key.</p>

<h2 id="left-hand-roots-and-shells">Left Hand: Roots and Shells</h2>

<p>Phase 1: Play roots only—G, F, Bb, Eb, A, D—on beat 1 of each chord change. Phase 2: Replace roots with shells:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cm7: C and Bb (root and 7th) or Eb and Bb (3rd and 7th);</li>
  <li>F7: F and Eb (root and 7th) or A and Eb (3rd and 7th);</li>
  <li>Bbmaj7: Bb and A (root and 7th) or D and A (3rd and 7th).</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep the left hand in the lower middle register. For more left-hand options, see <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-left-hand-voicings/">jazz piano left-hand voicings</a>.</p>

<h2 id="right-hand-comping-one-pattern-only">Right-Hand Comping: One Pattern Only</h2>

<p>Choose Charleston rhythm for the first week: chord on beat 1, short chord on the and-of-2, rest. Apply the same pattern on every change in the A section without soloing.</p>

<p>Voice-lead guide tones in the right hand. From Cm7 to F7, hold Eb while C moves to A (3rd of F7) or move both 3rd and 7th by step. Our <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-guide-tones/">guide tones article</a> details these moves.</p>

<h2 id="listening-before-playing">Listening Before Playing</h2>

<p>Hear how Cannonball Adderley with Miles Davis treats time on this tune—medium swing, clear quarter-note bass implication. Bill Evans recordings emphasize lush voicings. Pick one model for tempo and feel before you lock your practice tempo.</p>

<h2 id="first-solo-chord-tones-only">First Solo: Chord Tones Only</h2>

<p>On each chord, use only chord tones on strong beats (1 and 3). Example on Cm7: Eb, G, Bb, C. On F7: A, C, Eb, F. On Bbmaj7: D, F, A, Bb.</p>

<p>Rules for chorus one:</p>

<ul>
  <li>No scales—only 1-3-5-7 of each chord;</li>
  <li>Start phrases on beat 1 or beat 3;</li>
  <li>End each ii-V-I segment on a chord tone of the tonic (D on Bbmaj7, B on Gm7);</li>
  <li>Leave at least two beats rest per bar.</li>
</ul>

<p>On Am7b5-D7-Gm7, aim for C on Am7b5, F# on D7 (the 3rd), and Bb on Gm7 on downbeats. The half-step resolutions mirror the comping voice leading.</p>

<h2 id="adding-the-melody-later">Adding the Melody Later</h2>

<p>Learn the melody by ear from a recording rather than from a screenshot of notation in a blog post. Once shells and one chorus of chord-tone soloing work, add the melody in the right hand while the left hand keeps shells. This is the classic beginner arrangement path.</p>

<h2 id="tempo-and-metronome-plan">Tempo and Metronome Plan</h2>

<p>Week 1: quarter note = 72, roots only. Week 2: shells, same tempo. Week 3: Charleston comping. Week 4: chord-tone solo at 80. Use metronome on 2 and 4 throughout.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Ignoring the bridge and repeating only the A section;</li>
  <li>Playing different comping rhythms every bar—choose one pattern per chorus;</li>
  <li>Soloing with a full scale before hearing chord tones land on changes;</li>
  <li>Practicing only G minor and being surprised on an E minor gig.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="bridge-harmonic-detail-in-g-minor">Bridge Harmonic Detail in G Minor</h2>

<p>Bridge bars often move through sequential ii-V pairs. A typical bars 17–20 outline: Bbmaj7 for two bars, then Am7b5-D7 approaching Gm7. Treat each two-bar segment as a mini cadence—shell voice-leading on 3rds and 7ths without changing comping rhythm mid-bridge.</p>

<p>When the bridge modulates briefly toward E-flat major (Dm7-G7-Ebmaj7 in some charts), hear the G7 as V7 of Eb before the line returns to C minor territory. That motion repeats the same dominant pull you practice in isolation on Dm7-G7-Cmaj7, only with different roots on the page.</p>

<h2 id="performance-checklist">Performance Checklist</h2>

<p>Before calling Autumn Leaves on a gig: confirm key with the singer; agree intro length (often four bars of ii-V in the home key); decide whether the last A repeats or goes straight to tag. Pianists who nail harmony but forget form confuse horn players more than wrong voicings do.</p>

<h2 id="intro-and-ending-options">Intro and Ending Options</h2>

<p>Common intro: four bars of Am7b5-D7-Gm7-Gm7 in G minor, or rubato single-note line outlining the first chord’s 3rd and 7th. Endings often tag with Cm7-F7-Bbmaj7 motion even when the last A ends on Gm7—check the band’s preferred ending before the gig.</p>

<p>For solo piano, repeat the final ii-V-I slower with shells in both hands—no rubato tempo drift without metronome check afterward.</p>

<h2 id="transposing-for-vocalists">Transposing for Vocalists</h2>

<p>Singers may request E minor or B-flat minor. Move the entire template a semitone or fourth—do not rewrite voicings from scratch. Shells and guide-tone rules transfer; only finger geography changes. One transposition rehearsal before the gig prevents panic when the singer says “down a minor third.”</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Autumn Leaves is 32-bar AABC with repeated ii-V-I in major and minor.</li>
  <li>Prepare G minor and E minor concert keys.</li>
  <li>Progress: roots → shells → Charleston comp → chord-tone solo.</li>
  <li>Voice-lead 3rds and 7ths through Cm7-F7-Bbmaj7 and Am7b5-D7-Gm7.</li>
  <li>Learn melody by ear after harmonic foundation is solid.</li>
</ul>
