<p><strong>Summertime</strong> sits at the crossroads of opera, blues, and jazz — often performed in A minor or D minor with a slow 4/4 pulse and rich modal color. For jazz piano, the tune rewards minor seventh voicings, blue notes in harmony extensions, and sparse left-hand comping that leaves room for the lyrical character of the piece.</p>

<p>This guide focuses on harmonic structure, a minor-blues reading of the progression, voicing choices, and comping approaches. Melody lines are not notated here; use a lead sheet or recording for the actual tune.</p>

<h2 id="key-and-mood">Key and Mood</h2>

<p>Lead sheets commonly place Summertime in <strong>A minor</strong> (relative to C major) or <strong>D minor</strong>. The A minor version emphasizes a dark, blues-tinged tonality with frequent Am6 or Am7 sonorities and borrowed dominant chords from parallel major.</p>

<p>Establish the tonal center by holding Am7 (A–C–E–G) and adding the 11th (D) for a modern voicing: A2, G3, C4, E4 in the left hand with D4 in the right.</p>

<h2 id="16-bar-form-overview">16-Bar Form Overview</h2>

<p>Many jazz arrangements use a 16-bar form (not 32). Structure varies by edition, but harmonic motion often divides into two eight-bar phrases with similar cadential patterns. Confirm bar count on your Real Book page before memorizing.</p>

<p>At slow tempos (60–80 BPM), each chord may last two bars; at medium swing, one chord per bar is typical.</p>

<h2 id="opening-progression-in-a-minor">Opening Progression in A Minor</h2>

<p>A widely used harmonic outline for the first eight bars in A minor:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Bar 1–2: <strong>Am7</strong> or <strong>Am6</strong> (A–C–E–G or A–C–E–F#)</li>
  <li>Bar 3: <strong>Fmaj7</strong> (F–A–C–E) — subdominant color in minor</li>
  <li>Bar 4: <strong>E7</strong> (E–G#–B–D) — V7 in A minor</li>
  <li>Bar 5–6: <strong>Am7</strong></li>
  <li>Bar 7: <strong>D7</strong> (D–F#–A–C)</li>
  <li>Bar 8: <strong>G7</strong> (G–B–D–F) or <strong>E7</strong> — turnaround setup</li>
</ul>

<p>The Fmaj7–E7 motion (IV–V in minor) is characteristic blues-jazz language. Voice-lead E7's G# up to A and D down to C when returning to Am7.</p>

<h2 id="second-eight-bars-and-turnaround">Second Eight Bars and Turnaround</h2>

<p>Bars 9–16 often repeat material with slight dominant substitution. Common turnarounds in A minor:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Am7 – Dm7 – E7 – Am7</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Am7 – A7 – Dm7 – E7</strong> (borrowed dominant on A)</li>
  <li><strong>Fmaj7 – E7 – Am7 – Am7</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>The borrowed <strong>A7</strong> chord (A–C#–E–G) pulls toward D minor or D7, creating temporary major-minor tension central to blues harmony.</p>

<h2 id="minor-blues-interpretation">Minor Blues Interpretation</h2>

<p>Even when the chart is not a 12-bar blues, treat Summertime's harmony with blues tools:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Use <strong>minor pentatonic</strong> (A–C–D–E–G) for right-hand fills when soloing — see <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-pentatonic-scales/">pentatonic scales for jazz piano</a>.</li>
  <li>Add <strong>blue note</strong> G# as passing tone over E7.</li>
  <li>Comp with triplet-feel or 12/8 implication at slow tempo even in 4/4 notation.</li>
</ul>

<p>Compare with a straight 12-bar blues in A: Am7–Am7–Am7–Am7 | Dm7–Dm7–Am7–Am7 | E7–E7–Am7–Am7. Summertime's progression is more elaborate but shares IV–V–i cadential thinking.</p>

<h2 id="shell-voicings-in-a-minor">Shell Voicings in A Minor</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Am7:</strong> A1, C3, G3</li>
  <li><strong>Fmaj7:</strong> F2, A3, E4</li>
  <li><strong>E7:</strong> E2, G#3, D4</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7:</strong> D2, F3, C4</li>
  <li><strong>D7:</strong> D2, F#3, C4</li>
  <li><strong>G7:</strong> G2, B3, F4</li>
</ul>

<p>Practice descending bass lines: A → F → E → A → D → G → A in half notes while keeping shells in the right hand register (C4–G4).</p>

<h2 id="rootless-and-extended-voicings">Rootless and Extended Voicings</h2>

<p>For combo settings without a bass player covering every root:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Am9:</strong> G3, C4, E4, B4 (7–3–5–9 Type B)</li>
  <li><strong>Fmaj9:</strong> E3, A3, C4, G4</li>
  <li><strong>E7#9:</strong> G#3, D4, F4, C5 (Hendrix chord — blues color on V)</li>
</ul>

<p>The E7#9 voicing (G#–D–F–A stack) adds grit before resolving to Am7. Use sparingly at cadences, not every bar.</p>

<h2 id="comping-at-slow-tempo">Comping at Slow Tempo</h2>

<p>Summertime demands restraint. Effective approaches:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Whole-note comping:</strong> One voicing per bar, sustain pedal connecting common tones.</li>
  <li><strong>Offbeat pads:</strong> Short voicings on beats 2 and 4 only.</li>
  <li><strong>Arpeggiated spread:</strong> Break Am11 (A–C–E–G–D) across two hands in slow rolled rhythm — not a melody, harmonic texture only.</li>
</ul>

<p>Avoid dense Red Garland hits; the tune's character is lullaby and heat, not uptempo swing.</p>

<h2 id="modulation-to-d-minor">Modulation to D Minor</h2>

<p>Some vocalists prefer D minor. Transpose all examples up a perfect fourth:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Am7 → Dm7</li>
  <li>Fmaj7 → Bbmaj7</li>
  <li>E7 → A7</li>
</ul>

<p>Run the progression in both keys weekly. Functional labels (i, IV, V) transfer; note names shift.</p>

<h2 id="practice-routine">Practice Routine</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Play roots in half notes through 16 bars at 66 BPM.</li>
  <li>Add shell voicings; focus on smooth third-seventh lines.</li>
  <li>Introduce rootless voicings and E7#9 at bar 4 cadences.</li>
  <li>Comp with whole notes plus pedal; record and check for over-busy rhythm.</li>
  <li>Play along with a slow minor backing track in A or D.</li>
</ol>

<p>For play-along workflow, see <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-backing-tracks/">jazz piano backing tracks</a>.</p>

<h2 id="interaction-with-bass-and-drums">Interaction With Bass and Drums</h2>

<p>In trio performance, Summertime's slow harmonic rhythm gives the bassist space for pedal tones and small melodic fills. Pianists should leave register below C4 open when a bassist plays roots. Comp above the bass using rootless shapes — Am9 with G on bottom in left hand only if no bassist is present.</p>

<p>Drummers often use brushes on this tune. Align chord hits with brush sweeps on beats 2 and 4 rather than punching every downbeat. If your backing track uses sticks at a jazz-ballad volume, lighten voicing density so the ride pattern remains audible.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonization-options-optional">Reharmonization Options (Optional)</h2>

<p>Advanced arrangers sometimes replace static Am7 bars with Am6–Fmaj7–E7–Am7 minor-line clichés or insert tritone subs on dominant bars (Bb7 instead of E7 approaching Am). Apply reharm only when the melody note on each strong beat fits the substituted chord. On slow tunes, one reharmonized bar per eight-bar phrase is enough — density kills the lullaby character.</p>

<p>Compare borrowed dominant colors with the tritone substitution guide before altering bar 4 or bar 8 dominants in A minor.</p>

<h2 id="ear-training-with-recordings">Ear Training With Recordings</h2>

<p>Listen to multiple recorded versions — Billie Holiday, Miles Davis, and modern vocalists — without playing along for the first pass. Tap the harmonic rhythm: how many chords per bar in the intro? Does the bassist outline roots or pedal? Map what you hear to your chart before adding voicings. Discrepancies between recording and Real Book are common on Summertime; the recording wins when preparing for a gig with a specific singer.</p>

<p>Consistent daily touch matters more than marathon sessions when learning this harmonic language at the keyboard.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Summertime is commonly in A minor or D minor with 16-bar form.</li>
  <li>Harmony blends minor tonality with blues IV–V motion and borrowed dominants.</li>
  <li>Shell and rootless voicings work at slow tempos with sparse comping.</li>
  <li>E7#9 adds blues tension before returning to Am7.</li>
  <li>Practice in multiple keys and compare with 12-bar minor blues language.</li>
</ul>
