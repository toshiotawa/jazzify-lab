<p>A <strong>jazz piano turnaround</strong> connects the end of a chorus back to the top — typically the last two bars of a 32-bar AABA or 12-bar blues. The most famous pattern is <strong>I–vi–ii–V</strong>, but working pianists swap in tritone substitutions, diminished passing chords, and chromatic ii–V pairs to keep endings fresh.</p>

<p>This guide builds turnarounds in C major, transposes the concept through all keys, and provides comping drills you can apply tonight on any standard.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-a-turnaround">What Is a Turnaround?</h2>

<p>In C major, a turnaround occupies bars 11–12 of a 12-bar blues or bars 31–32 of many standards. Harmonic function: leave the tonic, create temporary tension, and land on Cmaj7 at bar 1 of the next chorus.</p>

<p>Basic changes in C (one chord per bar unless noted):</p>

<ul>
  <li>Bar 1: <strong>Cmaj7</strong> (I)</li>
  <li>Bar 2: <strong>Am7</strong> (vi)</li>
  <li>Bar 3: <strong>Dm7</strong> (ii)</li>
  <li>Bar 4: <strong>G7</strong> (V)</li>
</ul>

<p>When compressed into two bars: <strong>Cmaj7 – Am7 | Dm7 – G7</strong> or one chord per bar across four bars before the repeat.</p>

<h2 id="harmonic-function-of-each-chord">Harmonic Function of Each Chord</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7:</strong> Tonic — home base (C–E–G–B).</li>
  <li><strong>Am7:</strong> Tonic substitute (vi shares two tones with I); also heard as ii in G.</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7:</strong> Predominant — sets up G7.</li>
  <li><strong>G7:</strong> Dominant — resolves to Cmaj7 (guide tones B→C, F→E).</li>
</ul>

<p>The vi chord (Am7) adds color without leaving the key — A, C, E, G overlap heavily with C major tonality.</p>

<h2 id="shell-voicings-for-the-basic-turnaround">Shell Voicings for the Basic Turnaround</h2>

<p>Left-hand shells (root, 3rd, 7th) in C:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7:</strong> C2, E3, B3</li>
  <li><strong>Am7:</strong> A1, C3, G3</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7:</strong> D2, F3, C4</li>
  <li><strong>G7:</strong> G2, B3, F4</li>
</ul>

<p>Comp quarter notes through the four-bar loop at 80 BPM. Watch voice leading: E on Cmaj7 moves to C on Am7; C on Am7 moves to F on Dm7; F on Dm7 moves to B on G7; B and F resolve to C and E on the repeat.</p>

<h2 id="tritone-substitution-on-v">Tritone Substitution on V</h2>

<p>Replace G7 with <strong>Db7</strong> (Db–F–Ab–Cb). Db7 is a tritone away from G7 and shares the same guide tones (B/Cb and F). Resolution to Cmaj7 still sounds convincing.</p>

<p>Turnaround becomes: <strong>Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – Db7</strong></p>

<p>Full explanation: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-tritone-substitution/">tritone substitution for jazz piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="diminished-passing-chords">Diminished Passing Chords</h2>

<p>Insert <strong>C#dim7</strong> (C#–E–G–Bb) between Cmaj7 and Dm7, or use <strong>Abdim7</strong> as a passing chord between Am7 and Dm7. Diminished chords divide the octave symmetrically — each voicing can move up or down in minor thirds.</p>

<p>Example with passing dim: <strong>Cmaj7 – C#dim7 – Dm7 – G7</strong></p>

<p>See <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-diminished-chords/">diminished chords for jazz piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="chromatic-ii-v-chains">Chromatic ii–V Chains</h2>

<p>Replace static Am7 with a descending ii–V sequence:</p>

<p><strong>Cmaj7 – E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 – Cmaj7</strong> (each dominant resolves down a fifth or half step depending on spelling)</p>

<p>Or the popular <strong>Am7 – D7 – G7 – Cmaj7</strong> three-chord turnaround in two bars: vi becomes part of a ii–V toward IV.</p>

<h2 id="rootless-comping-shapes">Rootless Comping Shapes</h2>

<p>Combo comping without roots in left hand:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7 Type A:</strong> E3, A3, D4, G4</li>
  <li><strong>Am7 Type B:</strong> G3, C4, E4, B4</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7 Type A:</strong> F3, Bb3, C4, E4</li>
  <li><strong>G7 Type B:</strong> F3, B3, E4, A4</li>
  <li><strong>Db7 Type B:</strong> Cb3, F3, Bb3, Eb4</li>
</ul>

<p>Reference: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/type-a-type-b-rootless-voicings/">Type A and Type B rootless voicings</a>.</p>

<h2 id="turnarounds-in-other-keys">Turnarounds in Other Keys</h2>

<p>Move the pattern by whole or half steps. In F major:</p>

<p><strong>Fmaj7 – Dm7 – Gm7 – C7</strong></p>

<p>In B-flat major:</p>

<p><strong>Bbmaj7 – Gm7 – Cm7 – F7</strong></p>

<p>Practice around the circle of fourths: C → F → Bb → Eb → Ab → Db → Gb → B → E → A → D → G → C. One key per minute with metronome.</p>

<h2 id="rhythm-and-comping-patterns">Rhythm and Comping Patterns</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Half-note feel:</strong> One voicing change per bar — ballad endings.</li>
  <li><strong>Charleston:</strong> Syncopated hits on I and ii bars.</li>
  <li><strong>Anticipation:</strong> Play G7 on beat 4 of the Dm7 bar, resolve to Cmaj7 on beat 1.</li>
</ul>

<p>On a 12-bar blues in F, bars 11–12 are often <strong>Gm7 – C7</strong> (ii–V back to F) — same logic as turnaround, different key.</p>

<h2 id="applying-turnarounds-to-standards">Applying Turnarounds to Standards</h2>

<p>Songs ending on I often use turnarounds in the last two bars before repeating:</p>

<ul>
  <li><em>Fly Me to the Moon</em> — Am7–Dm7–G7–C in final A section</li>
  <li><em>Autumn Leaves</em> — different progression but same cadential purpose</li>
  <li><em>Take the A Train</em> — Am7–Ab7–G7–C or similar tag</li>
</ul>

<p>When jamming, agree with the bassist on which turnaround variant to use — unison hits on substituted dominants sound intentional; random clashes do not.</p>

<h2 id="practice-routine">Practice Routine</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Loop I–vi–ii–V in C with shells, 70 BPM, 5 minutes.</li>
  <li>Substitute Db7 for G7; same tempo.</li>
  <li>Add C#dim7 passing chord.</li>
  <li>Transpose to F, Bb, and G without stopping.</li>
  <li>Apply chosen turnaround on a full chorus of any 32-bar standard.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="turnaround-in-minor-keys">Turnaround in Minor Keys</h2>

<p>In C minor, a common turnaround variant uses:</p>

<p><strong>Cm7 – Abmaj7 – Dm7(b5) – G7(b9) – Cm7</strong></p>

<p>The Abmaj7 (borrowed from parallel major) brightens the return to minor tonic. Voice-lead Abmaj7's C down to B on G7(b9) when resolving to Cm7. Practice minor turnarounds separately from major I–vi–ii–V — jam callers switch keys without warning on minor blues and minor standards.</p>

<h2 id="turnaround-drills-with-metronome-grid">Turnaround Drills With Metronome Grid</h2>

<p>Set metronome to 70 BPM. Comp four-bar turnarounds in C with one chord per bar, then compress to two bars (two chords per bar), then expand to eight bars by doubling each chord's duration. Same harmonic content, three rhythmic densities — builds flexibility for ballads versus medium swing without learning new chords.</p>

<h2 id="applying-turnarounds-to-12-bar-blues">Applying Turnarounds to 12-Bar Blues</h2>

<p>Blues bar 12 often uses a turnaround identical in function to major-key I–vi–ii–V. In F blues: F7–Dm7–Gm7–C7 in the last bar or spread across bars 11–12 depending on the chart. Practice F blues with five different turnarounds from this article — muscle memory for endings transfers directly to jam sessions.</p>

<h2 id="play-along-integration">Play-Along Integration</h2>

<p>Loop I–vi–ii–V in C on a backing track at 90 BPM for five minutes at the end of each harmony session. Treat the turnaround as a mini-tune — it appears in dozens of standards and deserves the same repetition you give full 32-bar forms. Transpose the same loop to F and B-flat on alternate days.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>The classic turnaround is I–vi–ii–V — in C: Cmaj7–Am7–Dm7–G7.</li>
  <li>Each chord has clear function: tonic, tonic color, predominant, dominant.</li>
  <li>Tritone sub on V (Db7 for G7) and diminished passing chords add variety.</li>
  <li>Practice rootless voicings and voice leading in all keys.</li>
  <li>Match turnaround choice with bassist and tune style.</li>
</ul>
