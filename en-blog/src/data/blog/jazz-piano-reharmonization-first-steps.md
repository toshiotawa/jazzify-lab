<p><strong>Jazz piano reharmonization</strong> replaces or embellishes the chords on a lead sheet while keeping the melody and form intact. Beginners hear reharmonized ballads and assume random complexity. In practice, a small set of reliable substitutions—tritone subs, secondary dominants, diminished passing chords, backdoor ii-V—covers most introductory situations.</p>

<p>This guide explains when each substitution works, shows examples on ii-V-I and a turnaround, and warns where “creative” chords break the melody.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonize-only-when-harmony-is-solid">Reharmonize Only When Harmony Is Solid</h2>

<p>Play the original changes correctly first. If Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 is not automatic, substituting Db7 for G7 will confuse rather than enlighten. Master shells and guide tones from <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-shell-voicings/">shell voicings</a> and <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/how-to-build-jazz-piano-voicings/">voicing construction</a> before substituting.</p>

<h2 id="substitution-1-tritone-sub-on-v7">Substitution 1: Tritone Sub on V7</h2>

<p>G7 resolves to Cmaj7. A tritone away from G is Db—Db7 shares the same 3rd and 7th as G7 (B and F), swapped enharmonically (F-Cb on Db7). Replace G7 with Db7:</p>

<p>Dm7 | Db7 | Cmaj7</p>

<p>Voice-lead: F and C on Dm7 → F and Cb (B) on Db7 → E and B on Cmaj7. The bass moves D → Db → C—chromatic descent often sounds elegant.</p>

<p>Use on turnarounds: Dm7-Db7-Cmaj7 instead of Dm7-G7-Cmaj7. Do not use if the melody on beat 1 of G7 strongly implies G in the bass without preparation.</p>

<h2 id="substitution-2-secondary-dominants">Substitution 2: Secondary Dominants</h2>

<p>Before any diatonic chord, insert its V7. Before Dm7 in C major, play A7 (V7 of ii): A7-Dm7-G7-Cmaj7.</p>

<p>Before Em7, play B7. Secondary dominants add forward motion. Full theory is in <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/secondary-dominant-chords/">secondary dominant chords</a>. On piano, keep voicings small—A7 with C# and G (3rd and 7th) moving to Dm7 shell.</p>

<h2 id="substitution-3-diminished-passing-chords">Substitution 3: Diminished Passing Chords</h2>

<p>Between I and ii, insert #Idim7 (C#dim7 between Cmaj7 and Dm7) or use diminished on beat 4 approaching the next root. Cmaj7 | C#dim7 | Dm7 | G7 |</p>

<p>C#dim7 shares tones with A7b9—related to secondary dominant language. Melody must allow: if the melody sits on C on beat where C#dim7 appears, clash results. Check melody notes before inserting diminished.</p>

<h2 id="substitution-4-backdoor-ii-v">Substitution 4: Backdoor ii-V</h2>

<p>Instead of G7-Cmaj7, use Fm7-Bb7-Cmaj7 (“backdoor” to C). Fm7-Bb7 is iv-bVII in C—common in jazz ballads and endings.</p>

<p>Guide tones: Ab and Eb on Fm7 → A and Eb on Bb7 → E and B on Cmaj7. Softer than tritone sub, useful on beat 3–4 of a turnaround.</p>

<h2 id="turnaround-reharmonization-example">Turnaround Reharmonization Example</h2>

<p>Classic: Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 | Cmaj7 |</p>

<p>Reharmonized:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Dm7 | Db7 | Cmaj7 | Fm7 Bb7 |</li>
</ul>

<p>Or: A7 | Dm7 | Db7 | Cmaj7 | with secondary dominant plus tritone sub. Play one variation per chorus when comping—consistency helps soloists.</p>

<h2 id="when-substitutions-fail">When Substitutions Fail</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Melody note is the 4th of your new chord and clashes (avoid maj7 #11 on held melody 4);</li>
  <li>Bass line jumps awkwardly while melody is slow;</li>
  <li>Too many subs in one bar—pick one device;</li>
  <li>Form confusion on a jam session—communicate changes.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="practice-method">Practice Method</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Play original ii-V-I in C, F, B-flat for one week.</li>
  <li>Add tritone sub on V7 only for one week.</li>
  <li>Add one secondary dominant per standard (Fly Me to the Moon A section works well).</li>
  <li>Combine backdoor ii-V on final two bars of a 32-bar form.</li>
</ol>

<p>Apply to <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-autumn-leaves-practice/">Autumn Leaves</a> bridge only after A section originals are solid.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonization-and-tensions">Reharmonization and Tensions</h2>

<p>Substituted dominants invite altered tensions: Db7 with C (7th), F (3rd), Ab (b5), Cb (3rd enh.)—connect to <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/altered-scale/">altered scale</a> study after basic subs land on strong beats.</p>

<h2 id="coltrane-changes-preview-not-yet">Coltrane Changes Preview (Not Yet)</h2>

<p>You may hear Giant Steps–style key centers on advanced gigs. That is not beginner reharmonization—those are new structural progressions, not single-bar swaps. If someone suggests reharmonizing every bar of a ballad with tritone subs, decline until original changes are automatic for the room.</p>

<h2 id="communicating-changes-to-bandmates">Communicating Changes to Bandmates</h2>

<p>On a gig, say “backdoor on the last four” or “tritone sub on the second five” before counting off. Hand signals beat spoken Roman numerals mid-solo. Pianists lead harmony; clarity prevents train wrecks on turnarounds.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonizing-blues-turnarounds-only">Reharmonizing Blues Turnarounds Only</h2>

<p>Safest entry point: keep bars 1–8 original on F blues; experiment in bars 9–12 only. Try Gm7-C7 | Gm7-C7 | F7 | C7 | then F7 | D7 | Gm7 C7 | F7 D7 Gm7 C7 |. Soloists appreciate consistent bars 1–8 while you develop color in the turnaround.</p>

<h2 id="tritone-sub-voice-leading-checklist">Tritone Sub Voice-Leading Checklist</h2>

<p>Before using Db7 for G7, verify: top voice moves by step (C to B on Cmaj7 target); bass moves D-Db-C or chromatically; melody note on the substituted bar is not the lydian clash against Db7 unless intentional. Run the checklist on one tune for a month before adding secondary dominants in the same chart.</p>

<h2 id="writing-substitutions-on-the-lead-sheet">Writing Substitutions on the Lead Sheet</h2>

<p>Pencil original changes in black, approved subs in red, experimental subs in pencil only. At rehearsal, play originals first chorus, subs second chorus—band agrees which version to keep. Documentation prevents forgetting which substitution failed last time.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonizing-when-the-melody-is-static">Reharmonizing When the Melody Is Static</h2>

<p>Long notes on the tonic of a maj7 chord tolerate tritone subs on the preceding dominant best— the melody holds while harmony shifts underneath. Fast-moving melody bars need original changes or passing diminished only. Match substitution density to melodic rhythm.</p>

<h2 id="ballad-endings-with-backdoor-ii-v">Ballad Endings With Backdoor ii-V</h2>

<p>On the last four bars of a ballad in C, try Fm7-Bb7-Cmaj7-Cmaj7 instead of G7-Cmaj7 twice. The Ab in Fm7 and Eb in Bb7 add warmth. Hold the Cmaj7 shell C-B while the melody rests—common cinematic close on standards like Fly Me to the Moon.</p>

<h2 id="when-not-to-reharmonize">When Not to Reharmonize</h2>

<p>First chorus of a standard at a jam: play original changes so soloists can predict harmony. Save substitutions for your solo chorus or the last head out. Experienced players reharmonize together; beginners build trust with readable charts first.</p>

<h2 id="practice-log-for-substitutions">Practice Log for Substitutions</h2>

<p>Log which sub you tried on which tune and whether the melody tolerated it. “Db7 for G7 on Fly Me to the Moon bar 28—OK” beats vague memory. After ten logged trials you will know your personal safe subset.</p>

<p>Review the log before each gig where you might call a tune you reharmonized in practice—consistency matters more than novelty for the rhythm section.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Learn original changes before substituting.</li>
  <li>Tritone sub: replace V7 with dominant a tritone away.</li>
  <li>Secondary dominants: V7 of the chord you are approaching.</li>
  <li>Diminished and backdoor ii-V add color on turnarounds.</li>
  <li>Check melody and bass motion—one substitution at a time.</li>
</ul>
