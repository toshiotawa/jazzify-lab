<p><strong>Fly Me to the Moon</strong> is a gateway standard for jazz piano — moderate tempo, clear harmonic rhythm, and a familiar 32-bar A–A–B–A form in 4/4. The harmony moves through circle-of-fifths motion and classic ii–V–I patterns, making it ideal for practicing shell voicings, rootless comping, and swing feel without overwhelming chromaticism.</p>

<p>This guide covers the chord progression by section, left-hand shells and rootless shapes in C, comping rhythms, and practice steps. Melody notation is omitted; work from a lead sheet or recording for the tune itself.</p>

<h2 id="form-and-key-overview">Form and Key Overview</h2>

<p>Most lead sheets place <strong>Fly Me to the Moon</strong> in <strong>C major</strong>, though singers transpose freely. The form is 32 bars: eight bars per section, typically A–A–B–A. Harmonic rhythm is mostly one chord per measure in the A sections, with occasional two-chords-per-bar movement approaching cadences.</p>

<p>Before comping, play the roots alone in half notes to internalize the bass line: C → A → D → G → E → A → D → G in the opening eight bars (letter names refer to roots of each harmony).</p>

<h2 id="a-section-harmony-bars-1-8">A Section Harmony (Bars 1–8)</h2>

<p>A common chart for the first eight bars in C:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Bar 1: <strong>Cmaj7</strong> (C–E–G–B)</li>
  <li>Bar 2: <strong>Am7</strong> (A–C–E–G)</li>
  <li>Bar 3: <strong>Dm7</strong> (D–F–A–C)</li>
  <li>Bar 4: <strong>G7</strong> (G–B–D–F)</li>
  <li>Bar 5: <strong>Em7</strong> (E–G–B–D)</li>
  <li>Bar 6: <strong>A7</strong> (A–C#–E–G)</li>
  <li>Bar 7: <strong>Dm7</strong></li>
  <li>Bar 8: <strong>G7</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Bars 1–4 outline a long ii–V–I in C with a deceptive or passing approach through Am7. Bars 5–6 form a ii–V toward D minor (Em7–A7), then bars 7–8 return to Dm7–G7 resolving to C in bar 9.</p>

<h2 id="second-a-section-bars-9-16">Second A Section (Bars 9–16)</h2>

<p>Often mirrors the first eight bars harmonically, returning to Cmaj7 at bar 9. Some charts add slight variation in bars 13–16 — a secondary dominant to F or a minor subdominant. Verify against your lead sheet edition.</p>

<p>Practice voice leading between sections: end bar 8 on G7 with B and F resolving toward Cmaj7's C and E in bar 9. Keep the third and seventh of each chord as guide tones in the left hand.</p>

<h2 id="b-section-bars-17-24">B Section (Bars 17–24)</h2>

<p>The bridge typically modulates through a series of dominants and ii–V pairs — often visiting keys related by fourths. A frequent B-section chain:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Am7</strong> → <strong>D7</strong> (ii–V toward G)</li>
  <li><strong>Gmaj7</strong> or <strong>Gm7</strong> depending on arrangement</li>
  <li><strong>C7</strong> → <strong>Fmaj7</strong> (V–I toward F)</li>
  <li><strong>Fm7</strong> → <strong>Bb7</strong> (ii–V toward Eb or back to C via tritone routes)</li>
</ul>

<p>Exact spelling varies by Real Book version. Circle the ii–V pairs on your chart and label target tonics — this reveals the bridge's temporary key centers faster than memorizing chord names alone.</p>

<h2 id="final-a-and-turnaround-bars-25-32">Final A and Turnaround (Bars 25–32)</h2>

<p>Return to the A material. Bars 31–32 often use a turnaround back to Cmaj7 at bar 1 of the next chorus:</p>

<p><strong>Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7</strong> or <strong>Am7 – D7 – G7 – Cmaj7</strong></p>

<p>For turnaround vocabulary, see <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-turnarounds/">jazz piano turnarounds</a>.</p>

<h2 id="shell-voicings-in-the-left-hand">Shell Voicings in the Left Hand</h2>

<p>Shell voicings use root, third, and seventh (omit fifth). In C:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7 shell:</strong> C2, E3, B3</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7 shell:</strong> D2, F3, C4</li>
  <li><strong>G7 shell:</strong> G2, B3, F4</li>
  <li><strong>Am7 shell:</strong> A1, C3, G3</li>
</ul>

<p>Comp half notes through the A section with shells in the left hand and metronome on beats 2 and 4. Aim for smooth motion: the third of one chord often moves by step to the seventh of the next.</p>

<h2 id="rootless-voicings-for-comping">Rootless Voicings for Comping</h2>

<p>When a bass player covers roots, use Type A and Type B rootless shapes. Examples in C:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7 Type A:</strong> E3, A3, D4, G4 (3–5–7–9)</li>
  <li><strong>Dm7 Type B:</strong> F3, C4, E4, A4 (7–3–5–9)</li>
  <li><strong>G7 Type A:</strong> B3, E4, F4, A4 (3–13–7–9)</li>
  <li><strong>Am7 Type B:</strong> G3, C4, E4, B4</li>
</ul>

<p>Alternate Type A and Type B when the root moves by step or fourth to keep hand position compact. Detailed shapes: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/type-a-type-b-rootless-voicings/">Type A and Type B rootless voicings</a>.</p>

<h2 id="swing-comping-rhythms">Swing Comping Rhythms</h2>

<p>Start with <strong>Charleston rhythm</strong>: quarter note on beat 1, eighth note on the and-of-2, rest on 3–4. Apply to Cmaj7 for one bar, then move through Am7–Dm7–G7.</p>

<p>Next layer: <strong>Red Garland–style hits</strong> — short staccato voicings on beats 2 and 4 with occasional anticipations on the and-of-4 leading into the next chord.</p>

<p>Keep voicings under the hypothetical melody range (roughly C4–G5) so you leave space for a singer or right-hand line later.</p>

<h2 id="ii-v-i-drills-from-this-tune">ii–V–I Drills From This Tune</h2>

<p>Extract these progressions for isolated practice:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 (bars 3–4–9)</li>
  <li>Em7–A7–Dm7 (bars 5–6–7)</li>
  <li>Am7–D7–Gmaj7 (B section fragment)</li>
</ul>

<p>Transpose each to F, B-flat, and G — common singer keys. Review functional harmony in <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-ii-v-i-progression/">the ii–V–I progression guide</a>.</p>

<h2 id="practice-plan">Practice Plan</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Week 1:</strong> Roots and shells, half notes, 60 BPM.</li>
  <li><strong>Week 2:</strong> Rootless voicings, Charleston rhythm, 80 BPM.</li>
  <li><strong>Week 3:</strong> Full A section with metronome; add B section bridge.</li>
  <li><strong>Week 4:</strong> Play-along with backing track; trade fours with recorded bass and drums.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="tempo-and-style-variations">Tempo and Style Variations</h2>

<p>Fly Me to the Moon appears as medium swing, bossa nova, and even ballad in different recordings. Bossa versions often simplify harmonic rhythm — two chords per bar become one — while keeping the same A–A–B–A form. When practicing with a bossa backing track, favor rootless voicings with guide tones on top and avoid heavy left-hand root doubling that fights the bass pattern.</p>

<p>At ballad tempo (quarter = 60–72), whole-note and half-note comping with long pedal lets you hear voice leading clearly. Use the same chord roots as the swing chart but reduce rhythmic density until extensions (9ths and 13ths) lock in comfortably under a hypothetical vocal line.</p>

<h2 id="transposition-for-vocalists">Transposition for Vocalists</h2>

<p>Vocalists request Fly Me to the Moon in many keys — commonly C, B-flat, G, and F. Pianists should comp the same form in at least four keys without lead sheet dependency on letter names alone. Practice Roman-numeral thinking: vi–ii–V–I in B-flat is Gm7–Cm7–F7–Bbmaj7. Write one-page reference sheets with only numerals and roots for quick gig prep.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Fly Me to the Moon uses 32-bar A–A–B–A form, usually in C major.</li>
  <li>The A section features Am7, Dm7–G7, Em7–A7, and return ii–V motion.</li>
  <li>Shell voicings build foundation; rootless Type A/B shapes suit combo comping.</li>
  <li>Bridge modulates through ii–V pairs — label target keys on your lead sheet.</li>
  <li>Practice Charleston rhythm and extract ii–V–I fragments for all-key work.</li>
</ul>
