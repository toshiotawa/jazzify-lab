<p><strong>So What</strong> from Miles Davis's <em>Kind of Blue</em> introduced countless musicians to modal jazz. For piano, the tune boils down to two modes — <strong>D Dorian</strong> and <strong>Eb Dorian</strong> — over a bass line that defines the form. Comping is about color and groove, not functional ii–V–I chains.</p>

<p>This guide explains the harmonic structure, quartal and modal voicings, comping textures, and solo pitch collections. Melody notation is omitted; listen to the recording for the famous bass motif and head.</p>

<h2 id="modal-vs-functional-harmony">Modal vs. Functional Harmony</h2>

<p>In functional jazz, chords pull toward tonics through dominants. In So What, each section hangs on a single sonority — typically <strong>Dm7</strong> or <strong>Ebm7</strong> — and improvisers draw from modes rather than resolving progressions.</p>

<p>D Dorian is the D natural minor scale with raised 6th: <strong>D–E–F–G–A–B–C</strong>. Eb Dorian: <strong>Eb–F–Gb–Ab–Bb–C–Db</strong>.</p>

<h2 id="32-bar-form-overview">32-Bar Form Overview</h2>

<p>Standard arrangement structure:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Bars 1–8:</strong> Dm7 (D Dorian)</li>
  <li><strong>Bars 9–16:</strong> Ebm7 (Eb Dorian) — half step up</li>
  <li><strong>Bars 17–24:</strong> Dm7</li>
  <li><strong>Bars 25–32:</strong> Dm7 with turnaround figures in some charts</li>
</ul>

<p>Some lead sheets notate the sections as Dm9 and Ebm9. Functionally, treat each as a modal center — the bass player outlines D and Eb while the harmony stays static.</p>

<h2 id="the-bass-line-context">The Bass Line Context</h2>

<p>Paul Chambers's bass pattern (notated here as note names only for piano awareness, not as copyrighted melody): alternating roots and fifths in a repeating rhythmic cell. Pianists need not duplicate the bass but must not clash with it.</p>

<p>Comp in mid and upper register (C4 and above) while the bass occupies D2–A2 range. Avoid heavy roots in the left hand when a bassist is present.</p>

<h2 id="quartal-voicings-on-dm7">Quartal Voicings on Dm7</h2>

<p>Stacked fourths define the Kind of Blue sound. Examples in D Dorian:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Voicing 1:</strong> D3, G3, C4, F4, Bb4 (quartal stack from D)</li>
  <li><strong>Voicing 2:</strong> E3, A3, D4, G4, C5</li>
  <li><strong>Voicing 3:</strong> F3, Bb3, Eb4, Ab4, Db5 — use Ab and Db sparingly; they are b7 and b3 color tones</li>
</ul>

<p>Shift voicings horizontally rather than resolving vertically — modal comping slides between shapes on the same mode. Deep dive: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-quartal-voicings/">quartal voicings for jazz piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="quartal-voicings-on-ebm7">Quartal Voicings on Ebm7</h2>

<p>Transpose quartal stacks up a half step:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Eb3, Ab3, Db4, Gb4, Bb4</strong></li>
  <li><strong>F3, Bb3, Eb4, Ab4, Db5</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Practice switching between D and Eb sections without pause — the only change is shifting every voicing up one half step at bar 9.</p>

<h2 id="so-what-chord-pedal-and-color">So What Chord: Pedal and Color</h2>

<p>Pianists sometimes use the "So What" voicing — a minor 11th spread:</p>

<p><strong>Dm11:</strong> D2 (optional), F3, C4, E4, G4, A4 or Bb4 for the 11th</p>

<p>Hold this voicing and vary top-note rhythm rather than changing harmony every bar. Bill Evans's approach on the recording uses sparse movement — let the mode breathe.</p>

<h2 id="comping-rhythms-for-modal-jazz">Comping Rhythms for Modal Jazz</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Long tones:</strong> Whole-bar or two-bar sustained voicings — McCoy Tyner influence later, but Evans-style minimalism fits So What.</li>
  <li><strong>Offbeat stabs:</strong> Short hits on beats 2 and 4 in 4/4 swing.</li>
  <li><strong>Repeated modal cell:</strong> Shift the same quartal shape up and down the mode by step every two bars.</li>
</ul>

<p>Avoid functional cadences — inserting A7 or G7 into D Dorian sections fights the tune's design unless you deliberately reharmonize for modern arrangements.</p>

<h2 id="modal-solo-vocabulary">Modal Solo Vocabulary</h2>

<p>Over D Dorian, prioritize:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Chord tones: D, F, A, C (1, b3, 5, b7)</li>
  <li>Color tones: E (9), G (11), B (6 — characteristic Dorian note)</li>
  <li>Avoid leading-tone C# — that implies functional V–i toward D major</li>
</ul>

<p>Over Eb Dorian, same logic: Eb, Gb, Bb, Db with F (9), Ab (11), C (6).</p>

<p>Pentatonic shortcut: D minor pentatonic (D–F–G–A–C) works over D sections; Eb minor pentatonic over Eb sections. See <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-pentatonic-scales/">pentatonic scales for jazz improvisation</a>.</p>

<h2 id="relationship-to-other-modes">Relationship to Other Modes</h2>

<p>D Dorian shares notes with C major and F major — but thinking "C major" while soloing can weaken the D modal center. Aim your phrases toward D.</p>

<p>For broader mode study, read <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-modes-for-piano/">jazz modes for piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="ensemble-role">Ensemble Role</h2>

<p>With horns playing the head, piano comping is often minimal — pads and occasional rhythmic commentary. In trio without horns, you may imply the form with occasional bass-register D and Eb roots in left hand, but keep texture light.</p>

<p>Drummer plays straight-ahead swing or slightly straight eighths depending on arrangement; lock hi-hat pattern before adding voicing complexity.</p>

<h2 id="practice-routine">Practice Routine</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Play D Dorian scale two octaves hands together; repeat Eb Dorian.</li>
  <li>Comp quartal voicings on Dm7 for eight bars at 120 BPM — one voicing per two bars.</li>
  <li>Switch to Ebm7 at bar 9 without stopping.</li>
  <li>Solo one chorus using only chord tones plus the 6th (B in D, C in Eb).</li>
  <li>Add Bill Evans recording: comp along at reduced volume, match sparseness.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="from-so-what-to-other-modal-tunes">From So What to Other Modal Tunes</h2>

<p>Skills built on So What transfer directly to <em>Impressions</em> (D Dorian / Eb Dorian / D Dorian structure) and to <em>Maiden Voyage</em>-style sus voicings. The habit of horizontal quartal shifting matters more than memorizing tune-specific chord symbols. Once D and Eb Dorian quartal stacks feel automatic, learn one new modal standard per month using the same practice template — eight bars per mode before switching.</p>

<p>Avoid importing ii–V–I licks into static modal sections; functional dominants contradict the harmonic stasis that defines the genre on these recordings.</p>

<h2 id="time-feel-without-functional-cadences">Time Feel Without Functional Cadences</h2>

<p>Modal comping still requires pulse clarity. Practice with metronome on all four beats first, then remove beats 1 and 3 click while maintaining steady quarter-note internal pulse in your voicing rhythm. Drummers on Kind of Blue keep subtle swing on ride cymbal even though harmony stays static — mirror that feel with offbeat chord stabs rather than aimless whole-note pads.</p>

<h2 id="recording-yourself-against-reference-tracks">Recording Yourself Against Reference Tracks</h2>

<p>Record one chorus of comping along with Kind of Blue, then listen at low volume comparing density to Bill Evans. Most students comp too much. Aim for fewer than eight voicing changes per eight-bar section when first matching the recording's sparseness — increase activity only after achieving silence tolerance.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>So What uses 32 bars alternating D Dorian and Eb Dorian modal centers.</li>
  <li>Harmony stays on Dm7 and Ebm7 — no dense ii–V chains in the classic form.</li>
  <li>Quartal voicings and So What minor-11 spreads define the piano color.</li>
  <li>Comp with horizontal movement and long tones, not functional cadences.</li>
  <li>Solo using Dorian modes; emphasize the raised 6th as the characteristic color tone.</li>
</ul>
