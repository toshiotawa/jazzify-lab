<p><strong>Tritone substitution</strong> replaces a dominant seventh chord with another dominant a tritone away — same guide tones, smoother bass motion. Jazz pianists use it in turnarounds, ii–V–I reharmonizations, and walking bass lines to add chromatic color without abandoning functional harmony.</p>

<p>This guide explains why Db7 substitutes for G7, how to voice tritone subs at the keyboard, and where they appear in real repertoire.</p>

<h2 id="the-tritone-interval">The Tritone Interval</h2>

<p>A tritone spans three whole steps — six semitones. Between G and Db: G to Ab (1), Ab to Bb (2), Bb to Cb/B (3). Hence "tri" + "tone."</p>

<p>G7 contains B (3rd) and F (7th). Db7 contains F (3rd) and Cb/B (7th). The 3rd and 7th swap roles but remain the same pitch classes — the acoustic reason both chords resolve similarly to Cmaj7.</p>

<h2 id="basic-substitution-rule">Basic Substitution Rule</h2>

<p>To substitute for any dominant V7, move the root down a tritone (or up a tritone — same note):</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>G7 → Db7</strong> (resolves to Cmaj7 or Cm7)</li>
  <li><strong>D7 → Ab7</strong> (resolves to Gmaj7)</li>
  <li><strong>C7 → Gb7/F#7</strong> (resolves to Fmaj7)</li>
  <li><strong>F7 → B7</strong> (resolves to E maj/min depending on context)</li>
</ul>

<p>Both the original V7 and its tritone sub share the same tritone interval internally — hence the name.</p>

<h2 id="resolution-mechanics">Resolution Mechanics</h2>

<p>From G7 to Cmaj7: B rises to C, F falls to E — classic half-step resolution into the tonic chord.</p>

<p>From Db7 to Cmaj7: F (3rd of Db7) falls to E (3rd of Cmaj7); Cb/B (7th of Db7) falls to Bb or resolves to C depending on voicing. The ear accepts the pull toward C.</p>

<p>Practice voice-leading both dominants into the same Cmaj7 voicing: E3, G3, B3, D4.</p>

<h2 id="ii-v-with-tritone-sub-on-v">ii–V with Tritone Sub on V</h2>

<p>Standard ii–V–I in C: Dm7–G7–Cmaj7</p>

<p>Tritone sub version: Dm7–Db7–Cmaj7</p>

<p>The bass line descends chromatically: D → Db → C — a favorite walking pattern. Left hand plays roots; right hand comp rootless voicings.</p>

<p>Some pianists substitute on ii as well using related minor concepts, but beginner focus should stay on V7 replacement first.</p>

<h2 id="voicing-db7-for-piano">Voicing Db7 for Piano</h2>

<p>Shell: Db2, F3, Cb4 (enharmonic B3)</p>

<p>Rootless Type A: F3, B3, Eb4, Ab4 (3–7–9–13 of Db7)</p>

<p>Rootless Type B: Cb3, F3, Bb3, Eb4</p>

<p>Compare side-by-side with G7 shell (G2, B3, F4) — notice F and B/Cb are common tones.</p>

<h2 id="turnaround-application">Turnaround Application</h2>

<p>Classic turnaround in C: Cmaj7–Am7–Dm7–G7</p>

<p>With tritone sub: Cmaj7–Am7–Dm7–Db7</p>

<p>Or sub on vi's approach: Cmaj7–Ab7–Dm7–Db7 — double chromatic dominant slide. Use sparingly; one sub per turnaround often suffices.</p>

<p>More turnaround variants: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-turnarounds/">jazz piano turnarounds</a>.</p>

<h2 id="when-to-use-tritone-substitution">When to Use Tritone Substitution</h2>

<p><strong>Effective contexts:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Turnarounds and tags at end of choruses</li>
  <li>Long dominant bars where you want chromatic bass motion</li>
  <li>Comping behind a soloist who is playing inside the changes — sub adds color without confusion if rhythm section agrees</li>
  <li>Ballads at slow tempo where each altered dominant has time to breathe</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Use caution when:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>The melody strongly outlines the original dominant's natural 9 or 13 on a clash note</li>
  <li>Other band members are playing the original G7 explicitly — communicate visually</li>
  <li>Fast tempos with dense harmony already — too many subs muddy the form</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="extended-dominants-and-tritone-network">Extended Dominants and Tritone Network</h2>

<p>Chain of dominants descending by half step often alternates original and sub:</p>

<p><strong>E7 – Eb7 – D7 – Db7 – Cmaj7</strong></p>

<p>Each step is either a functional V or its tritone companion resolving down. Pianists use this in intros and endings — not typically entire choruses of a standard.</p>

<h2 id="tritone-sub-in-all-keys">Tritone Sub in All Keys</h2>

<p>Formula: if ii–V–I in key X uses V7 on scale degree 5, tritone sub root = flat II (half step above the tonic in major, or enharmonic equivalent).</p>

<p>In F: Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 becomes Gm7–Gb7–Fmaj7</p>

<p>In Bb: Cm7–F7–Bbmaj7 becomes Cm7–E7–Bbmaj7 (E7 is tritone sub of F7)</p>

<p>Drill ii–V–I with and without sub around circle of fourths at 60 BPM.</p>

<h2 id="relationship-to-secondary-dominants">Relationship to Secondary Dominants</h2>

<p>Secondary dominants temporarily tonicize non-tonic chords. Tritone subs can replace those dominants too: V/ii (A7 in C) → Eb7 (tritone sub of A7), resolving to Dm7.</p>

<p>See also <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/secondary-dominant-chords/">secondary dominant chords</a> for the functional foundation before substituting.</p>

<h2 id="ear-training">Ear Training</h2>

<p>Play G7 and Db7 in random order; resolve both to Cmaj7. Quiz yourself: which dominant was played? If you cannot tell reliably, the sub is working — guide tones dominate the ear's expectation.</p>

<p>Record comping on a blues in F: bars 9–10 Bb7–B7 (tritone sub of F7) → Bb7. Listen for chromatic bass Bb–A–Ab–G if walking.</p>

<h2 id="practice-routine">Practice Routine</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Voice G7 and Db7 rootless; resolve to Cmaj7; 10 minutes.</li>
  <li>Loop Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 then Dm7–Db7–Cmaj7; compare bass motion.</li>
  <li>Add tritone sub on turnarounds in three standards.</li>
  <li>Transpose exercise to F, Bb, G.</li>
  <li>Comp one chorus of blues with one tritone sub per chorus minimum.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="voice-leading-checklist-before-performance">Voice Leading Checklist Before Performance</h2>

<p>Before using tritone subs in public comping, verify each substitution in isolation:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Play original V7 → I, then Db7 → I (or equivalent sub).</li>
  <li>Confirm top voice of your voicing matches the melody note if comping behind a singer.</li>
  <li>Practice with a metronome on 2 and 4 — subs often rush because chromatic bass feels faster.</li>
</ol>

<p>One well-placed tritone sub per chorus sounds sophisticated; four per chorus sounds like an exercise.</p>

<h2 id="combining-tritone-sub-with-ii-v-extensions">Combining Tritone Sub With ii–V Extensions</h2>

<p>On Dm7–G7–Cmaj7, try Dm11–Db7–Cmaj9 — tritone sub only on the V bar while ii receives an extension. Top voice moves smoothly if Db7 uses F and Cb/B in the shell resolving to C and E on Cmaj9. Experiment in slow motion; record which voicings survive at 120 BPM without hand tension.</p>

<h2 id="historical-context">Historical Context</h2>

<p>Tritone substitution appears in stride piano, swing arranging, and bebop reharmonization — not a modern fusion device. Listening to Duke Ellington bass lines and Bud Powell comping reveals subs in context. Historical awareness helps you apply them on appropriate tunes rather than on every song regardless of style period.</p>

<h2 id="pairing-with-rootless-voicings">Pairing With Rootless Voicings</h2>

<p>After mastering Db7 for G7 in isolation, run ii–V–I with Dm7 Type B, Db7 Type B, and Cmaj7 Type A rootless shapes at 100 BPM. The tritone sub should feel like a bass-line color, not a separate harmonic universe — same voice-leading rules, different root name.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Tritone substitution replaces V7 with a dominant a tritone away — Db7 for G7.</li>
  <li>Both chords share the same 3rd and 7th pitch classes.</li>
  <li>Bass descends chromatically in ii–V–I contexts.</li>
  <li>Voice with rootless Type A/B shapes; resolve guide tones into the tonic.</li>
  <li>Use on turnarounds and long dominants; communicate with the rhythm section.</li>
</ul>
