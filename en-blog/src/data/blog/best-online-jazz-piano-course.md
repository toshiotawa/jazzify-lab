<h2 id="what-makes-a-jazz-piano-course-different-from-general-piano-lessons">What Makes a Jazz Piano Course Different From General Piano Lessons</h2>

<p>General piano courses teach reading, technique, and repertoire from the classical or pop tradition. A serious jazz course must go further:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Seventh-chord fluency</strong> — building maj7, m7, dom7, and half-diminished shapes in every inversion.</li>
  <li><strong>Functional harmony</strong> — understanding why a chord resolves where it does, not just what letter name to play.</li>
  <li><strong>Repertoire application</strong> — applying voicings and scales to complete standards, not isolated exercises.</li>
  <li><strong>Ear training</strong> — recognizing chord quality, guide tones, and short melodic fragments by sound.</li>
  <li><strong>Rhythm and time feel</strong> — practicing with swing subdivision, not just straight eighth notes.</li>
</ul>

<p>If a course lists "jazz piano" in the title but never comping through a blues or a ii-V-I in a real tune, treat it as introductory material at best.</p>

<h2 id="curriculum-checklist-eight-topics-a-strong-course-must-cover">Curriculum Checklist: Eight Topics a Strong Course Must Cover</h2>

<p>Before purchasing, scan the syllabus for these milestones. Missing more than two is a red flag.</p>

<ol>
  <li>Triads and seventh chords in root position and inversions.</li>
  <li>Shell voicings (3-7 or 7-3) and rootless Type A / Type B shapes.</li>
  <li>Major and minor ii-V-I voice leading in multiple keys.</li>
  <li>Blues form comping and a simple blues scale solo approach.</li>
  <li>At least one modal tune (e.g., <em>So What</em> or <em>Impressions</em>).</li>
  <li>Scale choices over maj7, dom7, m7, and half-diminished chords.</li>
  <li>Basic reharmonization or chord-substitution concepts.</li>
  <li>Three or more complete standards with comping and solo demonstrations.</li>
</ol>

<p>A course that jumps to "advanced voicings" without establishing shell voicings and voice leading usually produces students who know many shapes but cannot navigate a tune.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-evaluate-video-quality-and-teaching-style">How to Evaluate Video Quality and Teaching Style</h2>

<p>Watch the free preview lesson with these questions in mind:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Does the instructor <strong>play examples at multiple tempos</strong>, or only demonstrate once at performance speed?</li>
  <li>Are voicings shown on a keyboard diagram <strong>and</strong> heard in context with a bass line?</li>
  <li>Does the instructor explain <strong>why</strong> a note choice works, or only which notes to press?</li>
  <li>Is there a downloadable lead sheet or practice backing track included?</li>
</ul>

<p>Prefer instructors who reference recordings. A teacher who says "listen to Red Garland comp behind Miles on <em>So What</em>" is grounding theory in the tradition, not just textbook harmony.</p>

<h2 id="feedback-the-feature-most-courses-lack">Feedback: The Feature Most Courses Lack</h2>

<p>Video courses teach concepts well but cannot tell you that your G7 voicing is missing the third or that your left hand rushes beat 2. That gap matters because jazz progress depends on repetition with correction.</p>

<p>Options for getting feedback without a weekly private teacher:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Community forums</strong> attached to the course — useful but slow.</li>
  <li><strong>Monthly group coaching calls</strong> — better, but spots are limited.</li>
  <li><strong>Interactive apps with MIDI input</strong> — instant pitch and chord-spelling feedback on every attempt.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="matching-a-course-to-your-starting-point">Matching a Course to Your Starting Point</h2>

<h3>Complete beginner</h3>

<p>Look for a course that spends the first modules on keyboard geography, note names, and basic rhythm before introducing seventh chords. Avoid programs that assume you can already read treble and bass clef fluently unless you genuinely can.</p>

<h3>Classical pianist crossover</h3>

<p>You likely read well and have hand independence. You need rhythm, chord spelling by ear, and comping — not more Hanon. Seek courses with dedicated crossover modules or teachers who address swing feel explicitly.</p>

<h3>Self-taught hobbyist with gaps</h3>

<p>Choose a course with a diagnostic quiz or a clear level map so you do not replay material you already know. Focus on voice leading and repertoire application rather than re-learning triads.</p>

<p>One-time video courses between $80 and $200 are common for 20–40 hours of content. Subscription platforms run $15–$40 per month. Live cohort programs cost more but include direct access to the instructor.</p>

<p>Calculate value by cost per guided practice hour, not just total video length. <h2 id="red-flags-when-shopping-for-a-course">Red Flags When Shopping for a Course</h2>

<ul>
  <li>No audio examples — PDF-only content cannot teach jazz time feel.</li>
  <li>Every lesson uses the key of C; no transposition practice.</li>
  <li>"100 voicings" lists without connecting them to functional progressions.</li>
  <li>No mention of listening, transcription, or repertoire.</li>
  <li>Refund policy shorter than 14 days with no preview lesson.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="questions-to-ask-before-you-buy">Questions to Ask Before You Buy</h2>

<p>Email the instructor or check the FAQ for straight answers:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Does the course include backing tracks at multiple tempos?</li>
  <li>Can I access content after my subscription ends?</li>
  <li>Is there a community or forum for posting practice recordings?</li>
  <li>Does the syllabus show which standards are covered by name?</li>
  <li>Is MIDI keyboard practice integrated, or is it video-only?</li>
</ul>

<p>Vague answers to these questions usually mean the course was adapted from generic piano content rather than designed for jazz.</p>

<h2 id="building-a-course-into-a-complete-practice-plan">Building a Course Into a Complete Practice Plan</h2>

<p>A course provides structure. You still need daily habits outside the videos:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>15 minutes</strong> — technique and voicing drills from the current module.</li>
  <li><strong>15 minutes</strong> — ear training (intervals, chord quality, short phrases).</li>
  <li><strong>20 minutes</strong> — repertoire: learn one standard per month, comp and solo.</li>
  <li><strong>10 minutes</strong> — listen actively to one recording of a tune you are studying.</li>
</ol>
