<p><strong>Chord melody</strong> on jazz piano means one player presents both the tune and its harmony — the goal of solo jazz guitar adapted to eighty-eight keys with spread voicings, independent bass, and reharmonization choices. Done well, it sounds like a complete trio; done poorly, it muddies the melody under dense grips.</p>

<p>This guide explains chord-melody principles, voicing strategies, and a step-by-step arrangement process without reproducing copyrighted melody notation.</p>

<h2 id="what-chord-melody-requires">What Chord Melody Requires</h2>

<p>You need simultaneous control of:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Melody</strong> — usually top voice, singable and rhythmically clear</li>
  <li><strong>Harmony</strong> — chord tones and extensions supporting each melody note</li>
  <li><strong>Bass</strong> — root or logical bass line when no bassist is present</li>
  <li><strong>Rhythm</strong> — swing feel, space, and form awareness</li>
</ul>

<p>Solo jazz pianists from Art Tatum to Bill Evans to modern players all balance these four elements differently — density is a style choice, not a rule.</p>

<h2 id="start-with-the-melody-alone">Start With the Melody Alone</h2>

<p>Before harmonizing, play the melody from a lead sheet or by ear with correct rhythm and articulation. Identify which notes are chord tones versus passing tones on weak beats — only strong-beat melody notes need full chord support immediately.</p>

<p>Example process on any standard in C: sing the melody, mark beat 1 and 3 notes in each bar, note longest-held pitches — those earn richer voicings.</p>

<h2 id="basic-harmonization-rule">Basic Harmonization Rule</h2>

<p>Place the melody note on top of a voicing that contains it as a chord tone or acceptable extension:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Melody note E over Cmaj7 → voicing with E on top: G3, C4, B4, E5 (close) or spread: C3, G3, B3, E5</li>
  <li>Melody note F over Dm7 → F is the 3rd: D3, C4, A4, F5</li>
  <li>Melody note B over G7 → B is the 3rd: G3, F4, A4, B5</li>
</ul>

<p>If the melody note is an avoid note on a strong beat (e.g., F# on Cmaj7), reharmonize briefly or delay harmonization until the melody moves.</p>

<h2 id="spread-voicings-for-solo-piano">Spread Voicings for Solo Piano</h2>

<p>Spread voicings separate bass, inner chord tones, and melody:</p>

<p><strong>Structure:</strong> Root (or shell) in left hand low register → inner voices (3rd, 7th, extensions) in middle → melody on top in right hand pinky range (C5–A5 typical).</p>

<p>On Am7 with melody C5: A2, G3, E4, C5 — root, b7, b3, melody as top.</p>

<p>Avoid doubling melody pitch in inner voices unless intentional unison emphasis.</p>

<h2 id="block-chord-style-four-way-close">Block Chord Style (Four-Way Close)</h2>

<p>George Shearing and early jazz piano used four-way close: melody on top, three chord tones stacked directly below within an octave. Fast-moving melody lines get four-note blocks that shift in parallel.</p>

<p>On C6 with melody G: E4, G4, A4, G5 — melody doubled an octave below in inner voice (Shearing often doubles melody at the octave).</p>

<p>Block chords suit uptempo heads; spread voicings suit ballads.</p>

<h2 id="shell-melody-approach-for-beginners">Shell + Melody Approach for Beginners</h2>

<p>Simpler entry: left hand shell (root, 3rd, 7th) plus melody in right hand alone — three-note harmony plus single-note top line. Sounds thin but teaches harmonic rhythm.</p>

<p>Progression Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 in C with melody note on each bar's primary beat — shells in left, melody in right. Add one inner note in right hand when comfortable.</p>

<p>Shell reference: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-left-hand-voicings/">jazz piano left-hand voicings</a>.</p>

<h2 id="reharmonization-in-chord-melody">Reharmonization in Chord Melody</h2>

<p>Solo arrangers substitute chords for color:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Tritone sub on V: G7 → Db7 under melody note that fits (B or F on top)</li>
  <li>Secondary dominants: A7 before Dm7</li>
  <li>Diminished passing: C#dim7 between Cmaj7 and Dm7</li>
</ul>

<p>Each substitution must accommodate the melody on top — if tritone sub clashes, keep original harmony for that bar.</p>

<p>Tritone guide: <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-tritone-substitution/">tritone substitution for jazz piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="bass-line-integration">Bass Line Integration</h2>

<p>Without a bassist, root movement defines form. Options:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Roots on beat 1</strong> each bar — simplest</li>
  <li><strong>Walking bass</strong> between chord hits — see walking bass article</li>
  <li><strong>Shell bass</strong> — root and 7th only, melody independent</li>
</ul>

<p>Do not let walking bass obscure melody rhythm — register separation is critical (bass below C3, melody above C5).</p>

<h2 id="rhythm-and-space">Rhythm and Space</h2>

<p>Chord melody fails when every beat is filled. Rest on beats 2 and 4 occasionally; let melody breathe a cappella for one beat before re-entering harmony.</p>

<p>Ballad tempo (quarter = 60–80): whole-note and half-note harmonizations. Medium swing: mix quarter-note melody with syncopated chord punches underneath.</p>

<h2 id="step-by-step-arrangement-workflow">Step-by-Step Arrangement Workflow</h2>

<ol>
  <li>Learn melody and form (32 bars, AABA, etc.) from lead sheet.</li>
  <li>Write chord symbols above each bar; circle ii–V–I segments.</li>
  <li>Harmonize strong-beat melody notes only with spread voicings.</li>
  <li>Add passing harmonizations on weak beats where fingers allow.</li>
  <li>Insert bass roots or walking line in left hand.</li>
  <li>Add one reharmonization per chorus (tritone sub or dim passing).</li>
  <li>Record, listen for melody clarity, thin overcrowded bars.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="practice-without-a-full-arrangement">Practice Without a Full Arrangement</h2>

<p>Daily micro-drills:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Harmonize major scale degrees 1–7 as melody over Cmaj7 — one voicing per note</li>
  <li>Take four bars of ii–V–I; place given melody note on top of each chord change</li>
  <li>Play melody of any standard RH only; add one chord per bar LH shells</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="performance-preparation-checklist">Performance Preparation Checklist</h2>

<p>Before performing a chord-melody arrangement publicly:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Play melody alone from memory — harmony cannot rescue an uncertain line.</li>
  <li>Add left-hand roots only for one chorus — confirm form landmarks.</li>
  <li>Full arrangement at 75% tempo with metronome on 2 and 4.</li>
  <li>One run at performance tempo without stops.</li>
</ol>

<p>Solo jazz piano audiences forgive one missed inner voice; they notice lost melody rhythm immediately.</p>

<h2 id="drop-2-and-drop-3-voicing-concepts">Drop-2 and Drop-3 Voicing Concepts</h2>

<p>Arrangers drop the second or third voice from close position down an octave for guitar-friendly spreads that pianists borrow. From close C6 (C–E–G–A), drop-2 might place G an octave lower while keeping C–E–A in upper structure. Experiment on static maj7 chords before applying to moving standards — drop voicings clarify melody on top with less hand strain than dense close position at fast tempos.</p>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Chord melody combines melody, harmony, and bass in solo performance.</li>
  <li>Melody stays on top; voicings must contain melody as chord tone or extension.</li>
  <li>Start with shells + melody; advance to spread and block voicings.</li>
  <li>Reharmonize when melody permits; use tritone subs and dim passing sparingly.</li>
  <li>Leave rhythmic space — not every beat needs full harmony.</li>
</ul>
