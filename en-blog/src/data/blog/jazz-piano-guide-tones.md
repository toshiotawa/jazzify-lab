<p><strong>Jazz piano guide tones</strong> are the 3rd and 7th of each chord — the two notes that define a chord's quality and function. When you voice a progression using only guide tones, the inner voices move by step or semitone while the harmony stays clear. This is the foundation of voice leading, and it is the skill that separates mechanical chord memorization from musical comping.</p>

<p>This guide shows how to find guide tones on the keyboard, connect them through a ii-V-I in C and B-flat, and apply the same logic to a full standard.</p>

<h2 id="what-guide-tones-are-and-why-they-matter">What Guide Tones Are and Why They Matter</h2>

<p>Every seventh chord has four notes: root, 3rd, 5th, and 7th. The root tells you the letter name. The 3rd tells you major or minor. The 7th tells you major seventh, dominant, or minor seventh. Together, the 3rd and 7th determine the chord's function in a progression.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Chord</th><th>3rd</th><th>7th</th><th>Quality defined</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Cmaj7</td><td>E</td><td>B</td><td>Major 3rd + major 7th = major seventh</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Dm7</td><td>F</td><td>C</td><td>Minor 3rd + minor 7th = minor seventh</td></tr>
    <tr><td>G7</td><td>B</td><td>F</td><td>Major 3rd + flat 7th = dominant</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Am7</td><td>C</td><td>G</td><td>Minor 3rd + minor 7th = minor seventh</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>If you can voice just the 3rd and 7th, you can comp through any tune with clear harmony and minimal hand movement. Shell voicings add the root; guide-tone practice strips the root away to focus on inner voice motion. See our <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-shell-voicings/">shell voicings guide</a> for the three-note version.</p>

<h2 id="guide-tones-in-a-major-ii-v-i">Guide Tones in a Major ii-V-I</h2>

<p>The most important progression in jazz is ii-V-I. In C major:</p>

<p><strong>Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7</strong></p>

<p>Label the 3rd and 7th for each chord:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Dm7:</strong> 3rd = F, 7th = C</li>
  <li><strong>G7:</strong> 3rd = B, 7th = F</li>
  <li><strong>Cmaj7:</strong> 3rd = E, 7th = B</li>
</ul>

<p>Play F and C for Dm7. Move to B and F for G7. Move to E and B for Cmaj7. Notice:</p>

<ul>
  <li>C (7th of Dm7) falls a half step to B (3rd of G7).</li>
  <li>F is common to both Dm7 and G7 — it stays in place.</li>
  <li>F (7th of G7) falls a half step to E (3rd of Cmaj7).</li>
  <li>B is common to both G7 and Cmaj7 — it stays in place.</li>
</ul>

<p>Each chord change involves at most one semitone of motion. That economy is what makes jazz harmony sound smooth rather than jumpy.</p>

<h2 id="choosing-guide-tone-positions-on-the-keyboard">Choosing Guide-Tone Positions on the Keyboard</h2>

<p>Guide tones can appear in two positions relative to each other: 3rd on bottom (3-7) or 7th on bottom (7-3). For a given chord, pick the inversion that connects to the next chord with the least movement.</p>

<p>In C major ii-V-I, a comfortable middle-register voicing:</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Chord</th><th>Left hand (3-7 or 7-3)</th><th>Register (approx.)</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Dm7</td><td>F (3rd) + C (7th)</td><td>Below middle C</td></tr>
    <tr><td>G7</td><td>B (3rd) + F (7th)</td><td>Same register — F is common tone</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Cmaj7</td><td>E (3rd) + B (7th)</td><td>Same register — B is common tone</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Play this cycle slowly with a metronome on beats 2 and 4. Each chord gets two beats. The goal is to hear the falling 7th-to-3rd lines that define the resolution.</p>

<h2 id="guide-tones-in-a-minor-ii-v-i">Guide Tones in a Minor ii-V-I</h2>

<p>In C minor, the ii-V-I uses different chord qualities:</p>

<p><strong>Dm7b5 → G7 → Cm7</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Dm7b5:</strong> 3rd = F, 7th = C</li>
  <li><strong>G7:</strong> 3rd = B, 7th = F</li>
  <li><strong>Cm7:</strong> 3rd = Eb, 7th = Bb</li>
</ul>

<p>The voice leading pattern is the same: 7th falls to 3rd, common tones stay. The difference is the minor quality of the I chord (Eb instead of E, Bb instead of B). Practice the minor ii-V-I in C, F, and B-flat before adding it to tunes.</p>

<h2 id="connecting-guide-tones-through-a-standard">Connecting Guide Tones Through a Standard</h2>

<p>Apply guide tones to the first eight bars of "Autumn Leaves" in G minor (concert key):</p>

<p><strong>Cm7 → F7 → Bbmaj7 → Ebmaj7 → Am7b5 → D7 → Gm7</strong></p>

<p>Work through one chord pair at a time:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Label the 3rd and 7th of Cm7 (Eb and Bb).</li>
  <li>Find the guide tones of F7 (A and Eb) that connect with minimal motion from Cm7.</li>
  <li>Continue through each change, writing the 3rd and 7th before playing.</li>
  <li>When all seven chords connect smoothly, play the eight bars at 60 BPM with a metronome.</li>
</ol>

<p>For a full practice plan on this tune, see <a href="https://en.jazzify.jp/blog/jazz-piano-autumn-leaves-practice/">Autumn Leaves for jazz piano</a>.</p>

<h2 id="from-guide-tones-to-full-voicings">From Guide Tones to Full Voicings</h2>

<p>Guide tones are a stepping stone, not the final voicing. Once the inner voices move smoothly, add:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Root</strong> in the left hand (shell voicing: root + 3rd or root + 7th).</li>
  <li><strong>9th or 13th</strong> as a color tone when the guide tones are secure.</li>
  <li><strong>Rhythm</strong> — quarter-note comping, Charleston rhythm, or syncopated hits.</li>
</ol>

<p>The order matters. Adding extensions before guide tones are clear produces dense chords that sound sophisticated but hide weak voice leading. Build from the inside out: guide tones first, then root, then color.</p>

<h2 id="common-guide-tone-mistakes">Common Guide-Tone Mistakes</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Jumping to root position every chord change.</strong> Root-position voicings force large leaps. Start from guide tones and add the root only after inner voices connect.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring the 7th-to-3rd resolution.</strong> The falling 7th is the strongest voice-leading signal in jazz. If you cannot hear it, slow down and isolate the two notes that move.</li>
  <li><strong>Practicing only in one key.</strong> Move the ii-V-I through the circle of fourths: C, F, Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb, B, E, A, D, G. Weak keys reveal themselves quickly with guide-tone exercises.</li>
  <li><strong>Playing guide tones without time.</strong> Voice leading is a rhythmic skill as much as a harmonic one. Always practice with a pulse, even at 50 BPM.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="a-20-minute-guide-tone-practice-routine">A 20-Minute Guide-Tone Practice Routine</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>4 minutes:</strong> Major ii-V-I in C, F, and Bb — guide tones only, two beats per chord.</li>
  <li><strong>4 minutes:</strong> Minor ii-V-I in C, F, and Bb — same approach.</li>
  <li><strong>4 minutes:</strong> Add roots to form shell voicings through the same keys.</li>
  <li><strong>4 minutes:</strong> Apply to four bars of a standard (Autumn Leaves or Blue Bossa).</li>
  <li><strong>4 minutes:</strong> Comp with guide-tone shells over a backing track at a comfortable tempo.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="summary">Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Guide tones are the 3rd and 7th — the two notes that define chord quality and function.</li>
  <li>In a ii-V-I, the 7th of each chord falls a half step to the 3rd of the next; common tones stay in place.</li>
  <li>Choose 3-7 or 7-3 positions to minimize hand movement between chords.</li>
  <li>Practice major and minor ii-V-I in multiple keys before applying to standards.</li>
  <li>Add roots and extensions only after guide-tone voice leading is clear and steady.</li>
  <li>Always practice with a metronome — voice leading is rhythmic as well as harmonic.</li>
</ul>
